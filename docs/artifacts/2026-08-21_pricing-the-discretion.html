Pricing the Discretion

A four-step sweep-and-reclaim method was described as “something that could be coded.” It can. But writing the function signature forces roughly a dozen decisions that nobody has stated, one step’s own examples contradict its rule, and the finished program would not test the method at all — it would put a number on what the trader’s judgement has to be worth.

Count the free parameters

“Could be coded” is true in a way that misleads. Anything can be coded. The real question is what you must decide before it will run — and here that is about a dozen things, none of which appear in the screenshots.

Step 1 — “sweep of a prior swing extreme”

DecisionWhy it changes everything
Swing definitionFractal with N bars left, M right. N=2 and N=5 produce disjoint sets of levels.
Confirmation lagA swing high is not knowable until M bars later. Ignore that and you have lookahead.
Eligible levelAll of them? Only unswept? Only the most recent? Only older than K bars?
Penetration thresholdOne point beyond, or ≥X points, or ≥X·ATR. A graze and a poke are different populations.
DurationMust the sweep complete within one candle, or may it span several?
Lookback horizon40 bars back, or 400?

Six decisions. The level picked on each chart is one draw from the set those six define.

Step 2 — “enter where price closes back inside, at the swept level”

This step contains a contradiction, not merely a parameter. “Closes back inside” is a candle-close event; “at the swept level” is a price. Those are two different fills:

  • Market on the reclaim close — entry is the close, which sits some distance inside the level. The extracted entries would not match.
  • Limit resting at the level — entry matches the extraction, but the fill is conditional. You are filled only on setups that return to the level after reclaiming, and the ones that run away without you are disproportionately the winners. That is adverse selection built into the entry rule.

The extracted entries are clean boundary prices, which points at the limit reading. If so, the coded version must model non-fills, and the tradeable population is a biased subset of the signal population. Two further unknowns ride along: how many bars may pass before the reclaim, and does it require a close inside or only a touch?

Step 4 — “opposite end of the prior daily range”

Nearly parameter-free, which makes it the good one. Still unresolved: which day boundary — broker midnight, New York 5pm, UTC? On a 1h–3h chart that choice shifts the range, and daylight saving shifts it twice a year. And what is the rule when price is already outside the prior range as the setup forms?

The step-3 discrepancy

Step 3 — “stop just past the sweep wick” — is the least specified step, and it is the one that sets reward-to-risk, which sets everything else. “Just past” is a number nobody has stated: fixed points, a fraction of ATR, a fraction of the wick?

But before choosing among those, check the arithmetic on the three cited examples.

Discrepancy — the examples contradict the stated rule

Two of the three exemplar stops sit inside the swept extreme, at a price the market has already traded through.

For the AUDCHF long, “past the wick” means below 0.56688 — the stop is 19 points above it. For the NZDUSD short, “past” means above 0.59643 — the stop is 7 points below it. Only NZDCAD matches the description.

Exactly one of two things is true, and both matter:

1. The stated rule is wrong. The stops really are placed inside the sweep, where a simple retest removes them. That is a materially different — and far more fragile — risk model than the one described.

2. The cited extremes are the pre-sweep labelled levels, and the true wick extremes were never extracted. Then the reference point step 3 needs is not in the data at all, and step 3 cannot be coded from these screenshots.

Either branch lands in the same place: the step carrying all the leverage is the step whose own examples disagree. This is a discrepancy in the source material, not a resolved defect — resolving it requires going back to the charts.

A family, not a strategy

This is the thing to internalise before any code is written. Twelve parameters measured against five hand-picked trades is not a test — it is a search. Some member of that family will look excellent on any sample you hand it, and the fact that the rules “came from the charts” offers no protection, because the charts pin down perhaps three of the twelve.

The trap

Codeability that feels like rigour is the entry point to overfitting.

Code this in order to find the parameterisation that works, and you will find one. It will mean nothing.

The one test that survives the parameter problem

There is a way out, and it falls straight out of the fixed-target structure. For any parameterisation, the geometric baseline is known exactly — the hit rate a coin would produce at that reward-to-risk:

P_null = r / (r + R)

So instead of asking “does this configuration make money,” ask:

The question to ask instead

Does the realised hit rate exceed r/(r+R) — and does it do so across the parameter space, rather than at one chosen point?

This works because it is self-normalising. Move the stop and both the realised rate and the baseline move together. Change the swing definition and both move together. You stop measuring a parameterisation and start measuring whether sweep-reclaim carries information at all.

Report the distribution across cells — the median, and the fraction of cells above their own baseline. Never the maximum. If the sweep means something, most of the space beats its baseline by a consistent margin. If it is a thin ridge, it is a fit.

Build order: ascending parameter count

Do the cheap, decisive things first. This inverts the natural instinct, which is to start with the sweep detector because it is the interesting part.

1

The control — blind range-boundary reversion

Enter at the prior-day range boundary, target the opposite side, stop at X% of the range. ~2 parameters. Answers: is unconditional range reversion positive? This sets the bar step 1 must clear, and it is the highest-information, lowest-cost test on the list — probably an afternoon.

2

Step 4 alone — range and session definition

~2 parameters. Answers: does the target rule reproduce the labelled boundaries on the charts? This validates the extraction itself before anything is built on top of it.

3

Step 3 as a swept parameter, not a rule

1 parameter. Answers: where does cost-adjusted reward-to-risk actually optimise? Treating the least-specified step as a sweep dimension is more honest than inventing a number for it.

4

Steps 1–2 — sweep and reclaim, added on top

~8 parameters. Answers the only real question: is there lift over the control?

Step 1 is the entire hypothesis and it goes last, because everything before it is the null it must beat. If the control at stage 1 is already negative and step 1 has to manufacture the whole edge, you know the size of the miracle required before writing a single line of the sweep detector.

Three traps specific to this trade shape

Intrabar ambiguity is severe here, and biased in one direction

The stop is 8–35 pips; the target is 70–150. On 1h bars an enormous fraction of candles contain the stop distance, and many contain both stop and target. OHLC cannot tell you which came first. With the stop at roughly 12% of the target distance, resolving that ambiguity optimistically does not add a little noise — it manufactures an edge out of nothing, systematically. Either use sub-minute data, or assume stop-first, and state which.

Lookahead enters through the swing definition

A fractal extreme confirmed by M right-side bars is invisible until M bars after it forms. Mark swings across the full series and you have used the future to choose the level. This is the single most common way a coded sweep strategy produces a spurious result.

Costs must be per-instrument

Spread-to-risk runs from about 4% on USDCHF to roughly 35% on NZDCAD. A flat cost assumption is not a simplification — it is a thumb on the scale, and it presses in the flattering direction on exactly the trades that look best.

What coding can and cannot settle

Worth being clear-eyed about, because it determines whether the exercise ends an argument or starts one.

A coded steps 1–4 generates the unfiltered population. His record is the filtered one — after the sweep-validity judgement, after voiding. These are different objects. So if the code loses and he is winning, he can say “your version takes bad setups,” and he would be technically correct, because the discretionary gate is precisely the difference between the two populations.

The actual deliverable

Coding steps 1–4 does not test his method. It prices his discretion.

If the mechanical core returns −0.12R per trade net, then his sweep-validity judgement must be worth +0.12R merely to break even, and more than that to be worth doing. An unfalsifiable claim — “I only take the good ones” — becomes a quantified burden of proof with a number attached.

Two questions, two instruments, and they do not substitute for each other:

QuestionInstrumentCharacter
Is there a substrate?backtest the mechanical coreLarge n, fast, answerable
Is the discretion worth anything?forward pre-registrationSmall n, slow, no shortcut exists

Conflating the two is how every one of these conversations goes in circles.

A prior, stated so it can be wrong

Pre-registered before any code runs, since that is the discipline being asked of him.

QuantityPredicted
Gross expectancy of the mechanical corewithin ±0.05R of zero
Net after real per-pair spreads−0.05R to −0.20R
Realised hit rate vs r/(r+R)within ~2pp across most cells
Fraction of cells beating their own baselinenear 50% — coin-flip

Reasoning: the geometric null is exactly zero by construction; costs run 4–35% of the risk budget; and the stop sits in or beside the densest retest zone on the chart. For this to come out positive, sweep-reclaim must lift the hit rate by several points and pay the spread.

If the realised rate comes in clearly above r/(r+R) across most cells, the prior is wrong and there is something real in step 1 — and that would be worth knowing precisely because the fixed-target structure makes it unambiguous.
