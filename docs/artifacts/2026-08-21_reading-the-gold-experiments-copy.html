Reading the Gold Experiments

Two research programs ran against this codebase. One asked whether the system can tell when to enter a trade. The other asked whether smarter exits could rescue it. Here is what each one did, what it concluded, and exactly which file on disk proves it — with every trading term defined as it appears.

A trade, end to end

Before any of the findings make sense, here is the object everything else is about. Your system watches gold prices and occasionally decides to buy or sell. Each decision has a fixed anatomy.

Candle (or bar)
A summary of all price activity in a fixed time window, recorded as four numbers: the open, the high, the low, and the close.
M15
Fifteen-minute candles — each row in your data covers a quarter hour of trading.
OHLCV
The five columns of a candle: Open, High, Low, Close, Volume.

Critically, a candle tells you the extremes reached during those 15 minutes but not the order they were reached in. That single limitation causes real problems later, and one of this session's findings is entirely about it.

Long / Short
Long means you profit if the price rises; short means you profit if it falls.
Entry
The price at which you actually open the position.
Stop loss (SL)
A pre-set price at which you automatically close a losing trade to cap the damage.
Take profit (TP)
A pre-set price at which you automatically close a winning trade to bank the gain.
Risk distance
The gap in price between your entry and your stop loss — how much you lose per unit if the stop is hit.
R (R-multiple)
Profit or loss expressed as a multiple of the risk distance, so "+2R" means you made twice what you were risking.

R is the unit everything in both programs is reported in. It exists so trades with different stop widths can be compared on one scale. A 1R loss is the same "size" of loss whether your stop was $2 or $20 away, because you would have sized the position smaller in the second case.

Position sizing
Choosing how many units to buy so that a stop-loss hit costs a fixed amount of money regardless of how far away the stop sits.
Expectancy
The average R you earn per trade across many trades — positive means the strategy makes money, negative means it bleeds.

The one number that matters

Every conclusion below reduces to: is expectancy above zero? Both programs answered no, and the interesting part is why — the two programs give different reasons, and the second rules out the explanation most people would reach for first.

Your specific trade object

Most trading literature assumes one stop and one target. Your production code does something more elaborate, and this matters enormously because the repository had been measuring the wrong shape of trade for its entire history.

Partial fill / partial exit
Closing only part of your position and letting the rest keep running.
Runner
The portion of a position you deliberately leave open after taking partial profit, hoping for a larger move.
Trailing stop
A stop loss that moves in your favour as the trade goes your way, locking in gains.
Breakeven stop
A stop moved to exactly your entry price, so the trade can no longer lose money.

Your engine runs this sequence:

  1. Enter at the close of a specific candle.
  2. Place the stop just beyond that candle's low (for a long), plus a small buffer.
  3. Set TP1 and TP2 as multiples of the risk distance.
  4. If TP1 is reached, sell 50% and move the stop to the half-way point between entry and TP1.
  5. Let the remaining 50% run to TP2.

A naming trap in your own config

The config key is called partial_tp_breakeven_enabled, which reads as "move the stop to breakeven." The code does not do that. It moves the stop to the half-way point, which is already in profit. The key name and the behaviour disagree, and the code is what runs. This is documented as ontology node SEM-017 so nobody re-reads the key name and assumes breakeven again.

The consequence: your research tool forward_walk models one target, no partial, no trail. Your production trades two targets, a partial, and a trail. Every outcome-bearing result in the repo's history was measured on a different object than the one that actually trades. The new kernel multi_tp_walk closes that gap.

How we measure, and why it is hard

Costs are not optional

Spread
The gap between the price you can buy at and the price you can sell at — you cross it on the way in and again on the way out.
Commission
The broker's per-trade fee.
Slippage
The difference between the price you asked for and the price you actually got, because the market moved while your order travelled.
Swap / overnight financing
A daily charge or credit for holding a position past the market's rollover time.
Gross vs net
Gross is the raw price movement you captured; net is what survives after all the costs above.

A crucial mechanical fact, and it drives the entire second program:

Cost in R depends on your stop width

cost_r = cost_price ÷ risk_distance

Costs are a roughly fixed number of dollars. If your stop is tight, the risk distance is small, so that fixed dollar cost is a large fraction of your R. Tight stops pay several times more cost, measured in R, than wide stops do — for the identical broker.

Overlapping measurements

Lookahead (or look-ahead bias)
Accidentally using information from the future to make a decision in the past, which makes backtests look brilliant and live trading fail.
Block bootstrap
A statistical technique that resamples data in contiguous chunks rather than individual rows, because neighbouring rows are not independent.

If you simulate a trade at every candle with a 40-bar horizon, the trade starting at bar 100 and the one starting at bar 101 share 39 of their 40 forward bars. They are almost the same measurement. Treating 47,157 of these as independent would make results look about √40 ≈ 6× more certain than they are. Every interval in both programs is a block bootstrap sized to the horizon.

The trap of the drifting instrument

Drift
A persistent directional tendency in a market over a period — gold rose across this two-year corpus.
Beta (in this context)
Returns you get simply from being exposed to the market's direction, as opposed to skill in choosing when to trade.
Control / benchmark
A deliberately dumb strategy your clever strategy must beat, to prove the cleverness added something.

Because gold rose, any strategy that is long often enough will look profitable. So the benchmark is not zero — it is passive same-direction exposure: just buy and hold for the same number of bars. This control changed conclusions repeatedly in both programs.

Program one — can the system pick entries?

Every prior program in this repo was entry-first: propose a detection rule, measure what it caught. That couples statistical power to how often the detector fires, and it produced samples too small to conclude anything — one prior program got 47 trades, another got 1.

This inverted it. Simulate a trade at every single bar, both directions, keep the profitable ones, then ask what the system's indicators were saying at those moments.

Feature
A number computed from price history that the system uses to describe market conditions — a moving average, a volatility reading, a candle shape.
State
A categorical label the system assigns, like "trending" or "compressed" or "sweep detected."
AUC
A score from 0 to 1 measuring how well a number separates winners from losers, where 0.5 means no ability whatsoever.
0

One row per bar carrying every feature and state the repository declares — 47,197 rows × 124 columns. No such artifact existed; each layer previously lived in a different script at a different cadence.

The gate failed first — on my own bad test

I originally asserted that shifting labels by ±1 bar should collapse the signal to chance. It cannot. Adjacent labels share 39 of 40 forward bars, so they are genuinely correlated. My criterion assumed label independence — the exact assumption the whole program exists to reject. Rewritten to shift a full horizon; the ±1 behaviour is now reported as the autocorrelation measurement it always was.

377,256 simulated trades: 47,157 bars × 2 directions × 2 stop geometries × 2 tie-break conventions.

Tie-break
The rule deciding what happened when one candle touched both your stop and your target, since the candle cannot tell you which came first.

Source correction — I read the tie-break backwards

I read a comment in update_trade saying TP2 > SL > TP1 and took it as the effective rule. Checking the actual source, _intrabar_trigger_price collapses each bar to a single price and tests the stop first — inline comment: "conservative: SL before TP1 on a spanning bar." The backtest path was already conservative. The optimistic ordering exists only in an analytics sidecar the backtest never calls. Fixed in the source and the ontology, not just noted in chat.

Program one verdict

The vocabulary carries real information, and none of it is economically usable.

Information exists: combinations of states beat the average outcome at 6–8× what random shuffling produces. That is not a multiple-comparisons artifact — the permutation draws are tight and the gap is an order of magnitude.

Value does not: zero of 84 state cells clear actual profitability on any configuration. The handful of combinations that do clear zero are indistinguishable from the permuted noise.

Note the distinction that does all the work here: beating the base rate means losing less than average. Clearing zero means making money. The base rate is around −0.29R, so a rule can beat it convincingly and still lose on every trade.

Program two — could better exits rescue it?

Program one left one question: the information is real but does not cross zero. Is that because the exit is throwing it away, because costs eat it, or because the information is simply too small?

This is the natural place to look. Your production system has exactly one dynamic stop rule — the half-way trail — and whether it helps had never been measured in either direction. The entire rest of the category did not exist for your two-target trade.

A

Compute the ceiling before searching

MFE (Maximum Favourable Excursion)
The best unrealised profit a trade reached at any point, regardless of where it actually exited.
Capture ratio
What fraction of the available profit your exit rule actually converted into realised gain.

If even a perfect, all-knowing exit could not profit, no rule could, and the search would be pointless. So that was computed first — 35 seconds, before any policy existed.

When you simulate at every bar, every bar has favourable movement in some direction, so this gate can essentially never close on an unselected population. It ran, it was cheap, and it establishes only that the axis did not close.

On disp_bar — the geometry production actually uses — the half-way trail ranks 12th of 12 arms in all four combinations, and 12/12 again on the other tight geometry. Eight of eight, worst every time, with a delta stable to three decimals across horizons.

And it is still not significant. The delta is roughly 15–25% of one confidence interval, and the combinations share the same bars, so no p-value is computed from their agreement. The defensible statement: consistently the worst point estimate, on a grid that is entirely below the noise floor. Nothing was changed in your config.

Correction — I had the intrabar bias backwards

I wrote in a docstring and in the ontology that letting a stop update from the current bar's own high "manufactures free money at scale." Measurably wrong. Arming a stop sooner makes it tighter sooner — so the very spike that arms a ratchet becomes the bar that exits on it. On 4,000 synthetic paths, ratchet_0.5r was worse on 920 and better on 248; breakeven_at_1r was never better. Fixed in three source files and the ontology, and pinned by a test asserting the direction, so it cannot silently revert.

Then the correction produced a bigger finding

I had carried that as a synthetic estimate and re-measured it on the real corpus. Real candles have much fatter wicks relative to their close-to-close moves than random walks do, and the gap grew 2–3×:

0.196RMean ambiguity band from not knowing intrabar order
0.0212RTotal spread across all twelve policies
9–15×The ambiguity is larger than what it would measure

Because a 15-minute candle cannot tell you whether the high came before or after the low, any policy that reacts to the running extreme — every ratchet, every breakeven trigger — has an unresolvable range of possible outcomes 9 to 15 times wider than the difference between policies.

A ranking restriction, applied rather than noted

Not rankable from 15-minute candles: all ratchets, all breakeven rules. Distinguishing them needs tick data.

Rankable: do-nothing, your production trail, and time-decay rules — all triggered by levels or elapsed time, never by the running extreme. Time-decay's ambiguity band is +0.0003R, essentially zero, which mechanically confirms the cause.

This does not rescue anything: the causal arm is the optimistic end in 14 of 16 cases, and every policy already fails. True values sit at or below what was measured.

D

Does the exit interact with the entry information?

The decisive test. Two possibilities:

  • Level shift — a better exit lifts every market condition equally, so it never unlocks the information.
  • Interaction — some conditions respond differently, meaning a combined entry+exit configuration exists.
Paired test
Comparing two options measured on the exact same samples, so shared randomness cancels out instead of being mistaken for a real difference.

Since every exit was measured on the same bars, the question collapses to something simple: take the difference between two exits on each bar, and ask whether the market state predicts that difference. If the state tells you which exit wins, that is interaction.

Result

0 of 16 comparisons show interaction.

Ten came back as clean level shifts, six were indeterminate, none showed interaction. The smallest p-value anywhere was 0.10, against an attainable floor of 0.05 given 19 permutations.

Program two verdict

The exit is not the binding constraint.

1. Gross expectancy is ≈0 at the every-bar level (+0.0010R). An exit cannot create edge the entry did not contain.
2. Net = gross − cost, and cost dominates, set by stop width.
3. The dynamic-stop axis is inert, and most of it is unmeasurable from 15-minute candles anyway.
4. No interaction with the entry information.

So of the three candidate explanations — exit, cost, information size — it is not the exit. Between the other two it is both, and they are the same statement: the information is not large enough to clear the cost any real stop placement incurs.

The uncomfortable observation

Zero of 156 long configurations beat simply holding the position. The widest stop minimises cost and has gross ≈ 0, so "widen the stop" converges toward "hold" — whose limit on this corpus is gold's own drift: +0.23R over 96 bars where the best exit configuration returned +0.058R.

This is a measurement about this corpus, not a recommendation. Holding without a stop has unbounded drawdown — a risk the R-normalised comparison deliberately does not price. What it establishes is that the exit machinery subtracted from a passive baseline over this period, which is a fact about where engineering effort has been going.

What we did not prove

The limits matter as much as the findings, and I would rather you know them before you build on any of this.

  • One instrument, one timeframe. Everything is gold on 15-minute candles under one broker's measured costs.
  • Drift contaminated three separate readings. Gold rose across the corpus, which makes tightening stops help shorts and hurt longs — I mistook that for a policy effect once and caught it by testing across all groups. A non-drifting instrument is the obvious decontamination.
  • The holdout is unspent. 236 non-overlapping bars were reserved and never read. Thin for validating anything, but honestly quantified before mining rather than discovered afterwards.
  • Nothing was changed in production. No config, no promotion, no live behaviour. The production trail was measured, not modified.
  • Selecting bars by their outcome is lookahead by construction. These programs describe the past; they are not an edge and cannot become one without forward validation.
  • No finding was formally registered. Both write-ups are point-in-time analyses pending your sign-off.
